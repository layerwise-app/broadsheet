The great
Leek war

Aldmouth’s back-garden leek growers have spent 61 years sabotaging each other in the politest way available. This year somebody used a spade.

Dora Hallam at the bench

At ten past four on the morning of 3 September, Ted Arkwright was asleep in a deckchair beside his second-best trench with a torch in his lap and a flask of tea going cold. At twenty past four, by his own account, Margaret started barking.

Margaret is a nine-year-old border terrier with no record of barking at anything except the Ledger’s delivery boy. When Arkwright reached the far end of the plot the netting had been lifted on one side, neatly, and his best pair of Pendle Improved had been topped with a spade.

“You don’t do that by accident,” he told me, holding up the pale stump. “That’s somebody who’s been to a show.”

The Aldmouth and District Leek Club has 43 members, a constitution typed in 1963 and a show bench in the back room of the Lamb on Bank Street. First prize this year is a £60 voucher, a rosette and the right to say so for twelve months. A leek is judged by cubic capacity: length to the tight button, times the girth, measured with a steel tape by a man from out of town who is not told whose it is.

A lot of brown ale

What it costs to win is harder to measure. Arkwright runs two floodlights on a timer. His neighbour, Alan Pybus, keeps a camera in a bird box. Harold Tunney, the club’s secretary and its champion in six of the last nine years, will not say what he feeds his, though the chemist on Mill Lane will say that Tunney buys a lot of brown ale for a man who doesn’t drink.

“People think it’s about vegetables,” said Tunney, in the shed, with the door shut. “It’s about the ten minutes on the Saturday when you’ve got a bigger one than Alan.”

The club has had trouble before. In 1978 a bench of entries was found watered with paraffin. In 1994 two brothers stopped speaking over a half-inch of girth and have sat at opposite ends of the Lamb ever since, which is a distance of eleven feet. No one has ever been charged with anything.

42 people with a motive

This year the police have a crime number and a stump in a freezer bag. Constable Ruth Marr, who called on the Wednesday, says she has no suspects and 42 people with a motive.

The show is on Saturday. Arkwright’s remaining pair are under a tarpaulin with a length of fishing line around them, tied to a bell. He reckons them at 7lb 4oz between them and intends to sit up with them until then. Margaret, he says, has been given steak.

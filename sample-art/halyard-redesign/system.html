Pilots of
the Sado

Nine people bring every tanker up to Setúbal, and all nine are over sixty.

At 04:10 the launch leaves Tróia with one pilot aboard and a thermos that belonged to the previous pilot. Tomás Vidigal is sixty-four. He has taken the channel some eleven thousand times and he still reads it aloud, buoy by buoy, under his breath, the way other people say the rosary. The tanker is waiting beyond the bar with its deck lights on.

A ladder of rope and wood comes down the hull, nine metres of it, swinging. He climbs it the way you would climb the stairs at home. There is no younger way to do this, he says when he reaches the top, breathing through his nose. The training takes seven years and the port has not started anyone since 2016, when the budget was moved to a study of whether the port needs pilots. The study needed a boat. They borrowed his.

Up on the bridge the master is from Manila and hands him the ship without taking his eyes from the radar. Vidigal asks for dead slow and looks left, to where a heron stands on a sandbar that was not there in March. It moves, he says. The chart will find out in two years. Nobody on the bridge writes this down.

“The chart will find
out in two years.”

The Sado is not a difficult river in the sense of the Hooghly or the Humber. It is difficult the way a staircase in the dark is difficult, if somebody has been moving the stairs. The silt comes down from the rice fields at Alcácer and settles where it pleases. Four dredging contracts have failed to change its mind. By half past five the sky over the Arrábida is the colour of weak tea and we have passed the cement works. Vidigal has not looked at a screen once. He tells me about his knee, and the price of a house in Comporta. He tells me about the tanker that sat down on the Cachopo bank in 1998 with a pilot aboard who had also, that morning, been certain.

The port’s position, supplied by a spokesperson, is that nothing has ever gone wrong under the current arrangement, and that the current arrangement is under review. At 06:50 we turn off the Doca das Fontes with the tide on the ebb and a tug on each shoulder. Vidigal hands the ship back, signs a chit, and climbs down the same nine metres of rope and wood to the launch. Someone has refilled the thermos. He does not know who. In eleven thousand mornings he has never needed to find out, he says, and he hopes that nobody now starting out at the port has to learn the channel from an app, though nobody is starting out at the port. There are three more ships today, and his knee is no better, and the heron has moved on.

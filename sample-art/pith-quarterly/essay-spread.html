The Orange Is Mostly Not Orange

Weigh one, peel it, weigh the peel. Inês Tavora spends a January at the packing sheds in Silves and comes back with a bucket of marmalade and a grievance.

A navel orange from the Algarve weighs about 240 grams at the shed door. Peel it and you hold 170 grams of fruit and 70 of rind and pith, which is roughly the weight of a large egg, and which nearly everybody puts in the bin.

At Cooperativa do Arade they grade 11 tonnes on a good morning in January. Rui Salgueiro, who has run the line since 1998, keeps a bucket beside the belt for the fruit too scarred to sell. It goes to his sister, who candies it, and he is the only person in the building who has ever tasted the white part on purpose.

Pith is mostly pectin and a compound called naringin, which is why it tastes the way it does: dry, bitter, slightly soapy. Boil it three times in fresh water and most of the bitterness leaves. What stays is sweetness with a spine, the taste that separates a marmalade from an orange jam.

Salgueiro’s sister uses the third water for tea. She sells the candied strips at the Saturday market in Silves, €4 for a paper cone, and she cannot make enough of them, because the cooperative only pulls about 40 kilos of scarred fruit a week, and because she will not buy in.

The rest of the region’s rind, some 9,000 tonnes a year by the cooperative’s own count, goes to cattle feed or to a composting yard outside Lagoa. Nobody I spoke to thought this was a scandal. It is simply where peel goes. Over the next 20 pages we try to send a little of it somewhere else: into stock, into salt, into a cake that needs no butter, and into nineteen other kitchens that agreed to weigh their bins for a week.

ESSAY 2THE CHEMISTRY OF A COLOUR

What the Cobalt
Remembers

Marta Quintela, with analysis by Oluwaseun Adeyi

Every blue on a Lisbon wall is a ledger entry. The zaffre that a painter loaded on to a brush in 1738 had crossed the Erzgebirge in a sealed cask, been roasted, ground with sand at a mill near Schneeberg, and sold through Amsterdam at a price the Royal Treasury tried and failed to fix.1 Its trace elements travelled with it. Nickel and arsenic in the ore survive the kiln, and they can be read today from a chipped edge in about four minutes.

Between 2021 and 2024 we read them from 1,140 fragments held by the museum and eleven lenders. The results rearrange the attributions of two centuries. The so-called Master of the Ships, long placed in the Bairro Alto after 1720, was buying the same high-arsenic zaffre as the workshop of São Vicente as early as 1704.2 Panels that scholarship kept apart on grounds of drawing share a single barrel.

A second finding is more modest and more useful. Tin-glaze does not fade, so what looks like a pale decade is a poor one: blue thinned with lime and water when the Saxon supply failed during the war of 1756 to 1763.3 The figure-painting did not lose confidence. The workshops ran out of money, and they went on painting with less of the colour they could no longer afford.

This essay reads the catalogue backwards through its ink. Plates 41 to 88 are arranged by cobalt source and not by date, so that a reader may see, across forty-eight openings, how a market shaped a style. Date, workshop and hand are given in the entries, where a reader looking for them expects to find them.

2. Adeyi and Quintela, “Arsenic-to-nickel ratios in Lisbon faience”, Archaeomaterials Review 44 (2023), pp. 118–41.

3. See cat. 118–126, and the appendix of analyses, pp. 388–97.
